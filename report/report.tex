\begin{abstract}
We construct and analyse an opinion network from a 60-item ordinal attitude
questionnaire answered by 96 respondents across four thematic blocks
(technology, education, society, environment). The work has three parts.
First, we sanitise the data while preserving the distinction between two
different missingness mechanisms: structural blanks left when a respondent
abandons the survey, and scattered ``No Comments'' (NC) responses inside
otherwise complete questionnaires. NC is not recoded as Neutral and NC users
are not discarded; missing responses are instead recovered by question-wise
regularised proportional-odds ordinal logistic regression, validated by
artificial masking under both missingness patterns. Second, we show that the
response distribution is severely ceilinged---$79.8\%$ of valid answers fall
on the agree side and the grand mean is $+1.14$ on a $[-2,+2]$ scale---so that
raw similarity measures how agreeable a respondent is rather than what they
believe. Row-centring each respondent removes this component and reduces the
number of item pairs correlated at $|r|\geq 0.30$ from $327$ to $42$. Third,
we build correlation networks over the sanitised matrix and run
Deffuant--Weisbuch bounded-confidence dynamics on a respondent network. We
show analytically and by controlled experiment that the widely reported
cluster plateau at small confidence thresholds is an arithmetic artefact of
the five-point response grid, while the difference between the sparse network
and a well-mixed population is a genuine effect of topology.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Likert questionnaires are a natural setting for studying how attitudes in
different domains relate. Each item is answered independently, yet responses
depend on one another, and a network makes those dependencies explicit: items
become vertices and statistical association becomes weighted edges.

Turning a questionnaire into a network is not mechanical. Three decisions
determine whether the graph carries signal or merely re-describes the response
format. \textbf{How missing data is handled}: abandonment and deliberate
non-response are different mechanisms \cite{rubin1976inference}, and assigning
Neutral to a withheld answer invents data. \textbf{Whether response style is
removed}: when respondents agree with almost everything, every pair of items
correlates through shared agreeableness rather than content
\cite{paulhus1991measurement}. \textbf{What the network is compared against}: a
correlation-derived graph has transitivity built into its construction, so
clustering and modularity appear even in structureless data. This report
addresses all three, and reports the alternatives that failed alongside the
pipeline that survived.

\paragraph{Team and code.}
Team \textbf{Edge Runners}. All code, figures and data are at
\url{https://github.com/shreyash-lohare/dpcn_a1_opinion_network} and the whole
analysis regenerates from a fresh clone with \texttt{python run\_all.py}.

\section{Data and Preparation}
\label{sec:data}
% ============================================================

The questionnaire contains 60 items in four blocks of 15 --- technology
(T01--T15), education (E01--E15), society and ethics (S01--S15) and environment
(V01--V15) --- answered by 96 respondents. Five ordered categories are encoded
symmetrically as $\text{SD}=-2$ through $\text{SA}=+2$, which represents the
ordering without asserting equal spacing; a sixth option, \emph{No Comments}
(NC), is treated as missing. Internal consistency varies sharply by block:
Cronbach's $\alpha$ \cite{cronbach1951coefficient} is $0.87$ for environment and
$0.82$ for society but only $0.65$ for technology and $0.58$ for education.

\subsection{The response ceiling}

The most consequential property of this dataset is its ceiling.
Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling} shows $79.8\%$ of valid answers on the agree side,
$43.1\%$ \emph{Strongly Agree}, and only $7.0\%$ disagreeing. The grand mean is
$\mathbf{+1.14}$ on the $[-2,+2]$ scale. Two consequences drive every later
decision: item means barely discriminate, so the signal is in \emph{which}
statements attract disagreement; and two respondents look similar under any raw
similarity measure simply by both being agreeable.

The ceiling is not uniform. Environment ($\mu=4.39$ on the $1$--$5$ scale,
$89.5\%$ agree) and society ($\mu=4.28$) are near consensus, while technology
($\mu=3.99$) and education ($\mu=3.91$, $13.8\%$ disagree) retain real
disagreement --- and those two blocks also have the weakest internal
consistency, consistent with their items tapping several distinct opinions
rather than one settled disposition.

Ranked by disagreement, the twelve most contested items are seven
education, four technology, one society, none environment. Compulsory
attendance (E03, mean $2.06$, $70.1\%$ disagreeing) is the only statement the
cohort actively rejects; written examinations (E02, $2.84$) is next. The
pattern is specific rather than oppositional --- the same cohort agrees at
$94.3\%$ that curricula should be updated more often (E11). What is contested
is assessment and teaching format, not the institution. Technology behaves
likewise: teaching AI (T04) and AI as a learning aid (T02) score above $79\%$,
while AI-assisted diagnosis (T08, $3.08$) and autonomous vehicles (T09, $3.28$)
sit lowest. Since robots in hazardous work (T07) scores $82.4\%$, the objection
is to delegating consequential judgment, not to automation.

\subsection{Missing data and how it was recovered}
\label{sec:imputation}

Of 96 records, 85 are complete, 5 are entirely empty, and 6 stop partway. The
five empty records are dropped, leaving \textbf{91 respondents}; none is
dropped merely for being incomplete. That leaves $205$ structural blanks plus
$37$ NC cells, $\mathbf{242}$ in total. Dropout is \emph{monotone} --- a
truncated tail, not scattered gaps --- so later blocks rest on a slightly
smaller base.

\paragraph{NC is not Neutral.} Recoding a withheld answer as Neutral asserts a
response nobody gave. A two-way additive model
$\hat{x}_{iq}=\bar{\mu}+a_i+b_q$ fitted on the clean responses predicts $3.92$
at the 37 NC cells against $3$ for Neutral, so recoding would impose a
systematic $0.92$-point downward shift. NC use also shows no item- or
respondent-level structure (item-uniformity $p=0.27$; NC users' mean $4.01$ vs
$4.15$, $p=0.27$), so there is no basis for assigning it any fixed category.

\paragraph{Recovery by ordinal regression.} For target item $j$ the other 59
items form the predictor vector, and we fit one regularised proportional-odds
ordinal logistic regression \cite{mccullagh1980regression} per item, with
latent score $z_i=\beta_0+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i,-j}$ and
ordered thresholds $\theta_1<\cdots<\theta_4$; the prediction is the
maximum-probability category. This respects the ordering of the scale without
assuming equal intervals. Predictors that are themselves missing are filled
with the training-fold column median, computed per fold to prevent leakage, and
a ridge penalty controls the 59-dimensional fit.

\paragraph{Does it work?} The true values are unavailable, so we validate by
artificial masking under both mechanisms: \emph{scattered} masking hides 37
observed cells inside complete respondents (40 trials), \emph{suffix} masking
hides a contiguous tail after a simulated stopping point (15 respondents per
trial, 15 trials). Because the target is ordinal, MAE matters more than exact
accuracy: being one category out is not the same error as being four.

One caution carries forward. Comparing four NC treatments at $\tau=0.30$, the
correlation matrices agree at $r>0.94$ while their community assignments agree
at AMI as low as $0.337$ (dropping NC users, which discards 17 respondents to
remove 37 cells). \emph{Stability of a correlation matrix is not evidence that
a partition of it is stable} --- a warning that Section~\ref{sec:nulls} makes
quantitative.

\subsection{Row-centring}
\label{sec:centring}

Imputation yields a complete $91\times 60$ matrix, but not one that can be
correlated directly: with a grand mean of $+1.14$, a respondent who agrees with
everything raises all 60 entries together, which reads as association between
every pair of items at once. We replace each entry by its deviation from that
respondent's own mean,
\begin{equation}
x'_{i,j} = x_{i,j} - \bar{x}_{i\cdot},
\qquad
\bar{x}_{i\cdot} = \tfrac{1}{60}\textstyle\sum_{k} x_{i,k}.
\end{equation}
Centring is applied strictly after imputation and strictly before correlation.
Note that rank correlation does \emph{not} remove this bias automatically:
Spearman is invariant to a monotone transform of a column, not to an additive
shift that differs by row. Figure~\ref{fig:g3} measures how much it matters.

\subsection{What the network is}

\textbf{Vertices are the 60 questionnaire items. Edges are signed correlations
between items, computed on the row-centred matrix and retained with their sign.
Edges are weighted by default and binarised only where a metric requires it}
(path lengths, triad counts). Items were chosen over respondents deliberately:
a respondent network must be built by a neighbourhood rule such as
$k$-nearest-neighbours, and Section~\ref{sec:nulls} shows that rule manufactures
its own community structure, whereas an item network is thresholded directly on
a measured quantity and every vertex carries an interpretable label. Signs are
kept because a negative edge --- two questions dividing the cohort in opposite
directions --- is a stronger statement than mere association, and because the
structural-balance analysis is defined on the signs of triads. A second,
respondent-level network appears only in Section~\ref{sec:g12}, for the
dynamics experiment.

\paragraph{A note on respondent counts.}
Several reference values circulated during planning assume a pipeline variant
that drops respondents with more than 12 missing items, leaving 86. This
report does \emph{not} use that filter: all 91 non-empty respondents are
retained and their missing cells recovered by ordinal regression
(Section~\ref{sec:imputation}). Figures produced here therefore report
$n=91$, and small deviations from those reference numbers are expected.

\subsection{Graph 1: Missingness map and response distribution}
\label{sec:g1}

Figure~\ref{fig:g1} shows every cell of the raw $96\times60$ matrix as missing
or present, respondents sorted by missing count, beside the distribution of
per-respondent means.

\textbf{Findings.} The $542$ missing cells ($505$ structural blanks plus $37$
NC) are extremely unevenly distributed: five entirely empty respondents account
for $300$ of them, and the remaining $242$ spread thinly across the other 91 --
a rate of $4.4\%$. Below the cutoff the map is almost clean, with two visible
tails from respondents who stopped early. The right panel locates the grand
mean at $1.14$, with only one respondent below zero.

\textbf{Implication.} This justifies the retention policy directly: all but
five respondents answered at least $80\%$ of items, so there is no population
of badly-incomplete respondents whose removal would simplify anything, and a
count-based exclusion rule would discard usable respondents to remove very few
cells. The right panel is the first direct evidence of the acquiescence problem
that row-centring corrects.

\subsection{Graph 2: Item variance ranking}
\label{sec:g2}

Figure~\ref{fig:g2} ranks all 60 items by standard deviation on the
\emph{uncentred} matrix. Centring is deliberately not applied here: it changes
each item's variance and would answer a different question (spread after
removing each respondent's baseline) than the one asked (how divided the cohort
is on the statement as posed).

\textbf{Findings.} Item SD ranges from $0.50$ (E15, near-unanimous) to $1.18$
(E04, online learning). The five most contested are E04, E03, E02, T08, T07. By
block mean SD: technology $0.92$, education $0.88$, society $0.79$, environment
$0.72$.

\textbf{Implication.} Technology has the highest average variance, but the
\emph{sharpest} disagreements are educational --- three of the five most divided
items are E02--E04, all about assessment and teaching format --- and environment
contributes none of the top ten. This is a property of the instrument, not of
preprocessing: the questionnaire contains one settled domain, one moderately
settled, and two live ones. Consensus items carry little information for a
correlation network, since an item everyone endorses cannot covary with
anything, so the signal is necessarily concentrated in the contested tail.

\subsection{Graph 3: The effect of row-centring}
\label{sec:g3}

Figure~\ref{fig:g3} overlays all $\binom{60}{2}=1{,}770$ pairwise item
correlations before and after row-centring, on identical bin edges.

\textbf{Findings.} The raw distribution is displaced positive (mean and median
both $0.151$), with almost no pair appreciably negative. After centring it sits
on zero (mean $-0.004$, median $-0.008$) with positive skew $0.221$ leaving a
heavier right tail of genuinely co-varying pairs. At $|r|\geq0.30$ the surviving
pairs fall from $\mathbf{327}$ to $\mathbf{42}$ --- a reduction of $87\%$.

\textbf{Implication.} This converts the centring argument from an assertion
into a measurement: seven eighths of the apparent association in the raw matrix
is shared response style. Built on raw scores the network would have been
dense, almost entirely positive, and would have supported confident but
spurious conclusions. The emergence of negative correlations after centring ---
invisible in the raw distribution --- is also what makes the signed analysis of
Graph 11 possible at all.

\subsection{Graph 4: Real versus shuffled correlations}
\label{sec:g4}

Every analysis below runs on the same $91$-respondent, ordinal-imputed matrix,
so no part of this report silently uses a different sample.

Figure~\ref{fig:g4} compares the $1{,}770$ observed item--item correlations
against a null that destroys inter-item association while preserving every
item's marginal exactly. Each of $200$ replicates permutes \emph{every column
independently} and re-runs the full pipeline --- row-centre, then correlate ---
on the shuffled matrix. This detail is not cosmetic: one permutation applied to
all columns, or permuting rows, merely relabels respondents and leaves the
correlation structure numerically untouched.

\textbf{Findings.} Correlations from $n=91$ respondents scatter about zero with
SD $1/\sqrt{n-3}=0.107$ even when nothing is related. The permuted null
reproduces this at $\mathbf{0.106}$, against $\mathbf{0.139}$ observed. Real
structure exists but is modest, and lives in the tails:

selection a measured trade-off rather than a convention, and supplies the
chance curve that Graph 7 is read against.

\subsection{Graph 5: Eigenvalue spectrum against Marchenko--Pastur}
\label{sec:g5}

Graph 4 establishes that a noise floor exists but not how many genuine
dimensions sit above it. PCA cannot settle this: PC1 explains only $8.3\%$ of
variance, equally consistent with weak structure and with none.
Marchenko--Pastur theory \cite{marchenko1967distribution} answers it exactly ---
for a correlation matrix from $T$ observations of $N$ pure-noise variables the
eigenvalues fall inside an analytically known band, and anything above its upper
edge carries more structure than $T$ observations could produce by chance.

\textbf{Findings.} With $T=91$, $N=60$, $Q=1.517$ and
$\lambda_+=(1+1/\sqrt{Q})^2=\mathbf{3.283}$. The bulk of the spectrum tracks the
theoretical density closely and \textbf{exactly four} eigenvalues clear the band
--- $4.38$, $4.06$, $3.55$, $3.35$ --- carrying $\mathbf{25.6\%}$ of variance
(Figure~\ref{fig:g5}). The count is comfortable rather than marginal: the fourth
clears by $2.0\%$ while the fifth, at $2.86$, sits well inside the band.

Reading the loadings, the four modes are (i) environmental and social
commitment against technology governance and assessment tradition,
(ii) developmental optimism against regulatory caution, (iii) AI-enabled
education and industry against environmental stewardship, and (iv) systemic
technological action against individual and community obligation. Modes (i) and
(iii) split cleanly along topic blocks; (ii) and (iv) cut across them and are
offered as readings of the loadings rather than established constructs.

\textbf{Implication.} Three quarters of this correlation matrix is sampling
noise. That disciplines everything downstream: the network is rebuilt from the
four significant modes alone, with the diagonal restored to unity, so later
community and balance analyses operate on the part of the matrix that $91$
respondents can actually support.

\subsection{Graph 7: Threshold and percolation sweep}
\label{sec:g7}

Reporting a network at one threshold invites the objection that the threshold
was chosen to produce the result. Figure~\ref{fig:g7} answers it by rebuilding
the signed graph at all $46$ cutoffs from $0.05$ to $0.50$ and tracking edge
count, giant component, fragmentation and Louvain modularity
\cite{blondel2008fast,newman2006modularity} across the range, so the chosen
value is visibly a point on a curve. The sweep also absorbs the
giant-component analysis entirely.

\textbf{Findings.} The two constraints pull opposite ways: raising the
threshold lowers the chance fraction from Graph 4, but the graph eventually
shatters --- by $|r|>0.27$ the giant component holds under half the items. We
therefore state the rule explicitly: \emph{the highest cutoff at which the
giant component still spans more than two thirds of the items and mean degree
remains above $4$}, so community and path metrics stay meaningful. Since
spectral denoising has already removed the sampling noise the chance column
measures, raising the cutoff further would penalise the same noise twice. This
selects $\boldsymbol{|r|>0.22}$: $\mathbf{131}$ edges ($68$ positive, $63$
negative), mean degree $4.37$, a giant component of $50$ of $60$ items
($83\%$), modularity $Q=0.472$. At the next step, $|r|>0.23$, mean degree falls
to $3.47$ and the rule stops being satisfied.

Two independent controls agree there despite sharing no machinery. The
permutation null implies the raw graph's $205$ edges contain about $67$ chance
edges, leaving roughly $138$ real; spectral denoising, which never sees the
null, retains $131$ --- a $5\%$ difference.

\textbf{Implication.} Panel (d) is the cautionary one. Modularity climbs
steadily to $0.72$ as the threshold rises, but by then the graph is $43$
components with $38$ isolated items. Quoting a single high modularity without
the sweep beside it would report fragmentation as community structure --- the
exact error the null models of Section~\ref{sec:nulls} are designed to catch.

\subsection{Graph 9: Topic-block connectivity}
\label{sec:g9}

The questionnaire arrives with its own partition --- fifteen items each for
technology, education, society and environment --- which is an editorial claim
about what belongs together. Whether it is also a \emph{statistical} one should
be asked before any community result is interpreted against it. We collapse the
$60\times60$ correlation matrix into a $4\times4$ block matrix, averaging the
$105$ \emph{distinct} within-block pairs on the diagonal (self-correlations
excluded: keeping the unit diagonal would manufacture the very block structure
being tested). Significance comes from a \textbf{block-label permutation} ---
the matrix is held fixed and the T/E/S/V labels reshuffled over $5{,}000$ seeded
draws --- which needs no threshold and is immune to any constant offset in the
matrix.

\textbf{Findings.} Row-centring is what makes the design visible at all. On the
raw matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:g9}, left) all sixteen cells are positive
($+0.041$ to $+0.377$): acquiescence makes every topic look connected to every
other. After centring, the sign pattern separates cleanly --- \textbf{every
diagonal cell positive, every off-diagonal negative}. Within-block mean
$r=+0.0746$ against between-block $-0.0282$ gives a gap of $\mathbf{+0.1027}$
against a null of $-0.00003\pm0.0077$: $\mathbf{z=13.4}$, with no permutation in
$5{,}000$ reaching the observed value ($p<0.0002$).

The blocks are not equally coherent: environment $+0.136$, technology $+0.066$,
education $+0.054$, society/ethics $+0.042$. Environment is more than three
times as internally consistent as ethics. S--V is the one seam, at $-0.004$ the
only pair not clearly separated and the strongest off-diagonal cell in the raw
matrix ($+0.256$).

\textbf{Implication.} The thematic design is statistically real, so a later
community that aligns with a block is recovering something the instrument built
in, while one that cuts across blocks is the more informative object --- which
is exactly what Graph 10 finds (NMI $0.205$). The uneven diagonal sharpens
this: environmental attitudes travel as a package, whereas ethics items are
close to independent, so respondents hold ethical positions issue by issue
rather than as a doctrine. One caveat: the negative off-diagonals do \emph{not}
mean topics oppose one another. Row-centring forces each row to sum to zero,
pushing the grand mean of all $1{,}770$ correlations to $-0.0038$; the values
should be read relative to the diagonal, not as opposition.

\subsection{Graph 10: Communities against null models}
\label{sec:g10}

Figure~\ref{fig:g10} compares the community structure of the item network at
$|r|>0.22$ against three nulls: degree-preserving rewiring
\cite{maslov2002specificity}, an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph, and a
column-permutation null that scrambles responses independently per item.
Figure~\ref{fig:g10alg} compares three detection algorithms on the same graph.

\textbf{Which algorithm.} We began with Louvain \cite{blondel2008fast}, which
found 15 communities at $Q=0.472$. Louvain has a documented flaw: its greedy
aggregation phase can leave communities internally disconnected, capping
partition quality \cite{traag2019louvain}. Leiden adds a refinement phase that
guarantees well-connected communities and found a strictly better partition,
$Q=0.491$, again at $k=15$. Girvan-Newman \cite{newman2006modularity}, a
divisive method that repeatedly removes the highest-betweenness edge, peaks at
$Q=0.447$ --- also at $k=15$ --- and then declines monotonically. That all three
strategies converge on the same 15 communities makes the partition size a
robust feature rather than an artefact of one optimiser, so Leiden is used for
the primary analysis.

\textbf{And then the nulls disagree.} Leiden's $Q=0.491$ clears the
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi null ($0.420\pm0.016$, $z=4.46$) and the rewiring null
($0.333\pm0.013$, $z=12.13$) decisively. Against the column-permutation null it
does not merely fail, it falls \emph{below} it: $0.629\pm0.044$, $z=-3.14$,
$p=1.000$. Scrambled data pushed through the identical pipeline produces
\emph{more} modular graphs than the real data does. This is the same verdict the
respondent network returns in Section~\ref{sec:nulls}, reached independently on
a different network: the modularity is manufactured by thresholding a
correlation matrix estimated from 91 respondents, not carried by the opinions.

\subsection{Graph 11: Structural balance}

Figure~\ref{fig:g11} tracks the fraction of balanced triads in the signed item network as the edge-inclusion threshold $|r|$ tightens, comparing it against a sign-permutation null model \cite{cartwright1956structural}.

\textbf{Findings and Implication.} The observed fraction of balanced triads is remarkably high and strengthens monotonically as noise is filtered out. At $|r|>0.15$, $99.1\%$ of triads are balanced. At $|r|>0.20$ this rises to $99.5\%$, and for $|r| \ge 0.22$, all triads ($100\%$) are balanced. The sign-permutation null stays flat at roughly $50\%$ across all thresholds. That the balance fraction approaches unity as weak edges are removed constitutes strong evidence that the network's local topology reflects genuine structural balance, rather than random assortment.

\subsection{Graph 12: Opinion dynamics}
\label{sec:g12}

This is the only analysis involving an actual process; everything above
describes a fixed dataset. Opinions belong to respondents, so the vertices here
are \emph{respondents}. From the row-centred matrix we connect each respondent
to their $k=4$ most similar peers, symmetrised by union --- mutual $k$NN
fragments this data, and disconnected components cannot merge at any
confidence threshold, which would inflate the cluster count for reasons
unrelated to the dynamics. The result is $91$ nodes, $298$ edges, one connected
component.

Opinions $x_i\in[0,1]$ are seeded from E03 (compulsory attendance, the most
contested item), rescaled by $s(x)=(x+2)/4$. At each step a random edge is
drawn and the pair moves to its midpoint if they are close enough
\cite{deffuant2000mixing}:
\begin{equation}
|x_u - x_v| < \varepsilon
\ \Longrightarrow\
x_u,x_v \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{2}\left(x_u + x_v\right).
\end{equation}
We sweep $\varepsilon$ from $0.05$ to $0.60$, 20 seeded repetitions each.

\textbf{Findings.} The cluster count is exactly $5.00\pm0.00$ for every
$\varepsilon\leq0.25$, then falls to $4.45$ at $0.30$, $3.35$ at $0.40$ and
$2.00$ at $0.50$ (Figure~\ref{fig:g12}).

\textbf{The plateau is an artefact --- provably.} Under $s(x)=(x+2)/4$ the five
categories map to $\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1\}$, so any two distinct
opinions differ by at least $\tfrac14$. For $\varepsilon\leq0.25$ the merge
condition is therefore \emph{never} satisfied: the initial state is a fixed
point and the final count equals the number of occupied categories, on any
graph whatsoever. Three checks confirm it empirically. The plateau has
\emph{zero} variance across 20 seeded runs, which no emergent outcome would. A
complete graph --- a maximally different topology --- returns the identical
$5.00\pm0.00$. And jittering the seeds by $\pm0.125$, destroying the grid while
preserving the distribution, removes the plateau entirely
($35.95\rightarrow8.95$ clusters across $\varepsilon=0.05\ldots0.25$). The five
clusters are the original response categories failing to merge, not opinion
groups forming, and the break at $\varepsilon=0.30$ is arithmetic rather than a
discovered threshold.

\textbf{What is not an artefact.} Above $\varepsilon=0.25$ the comparison is
meaningful. An identically seeded complete graph --- the same dynamics with
topology removed --- collapses to about $2$ clusters across
$\varepsilon=0.30$--$0.45$, while the sparse $k$NN network sustains
$3.15$--$4.45$. \emph{Network structure sustains opinion diversity that a
well-mixed population loses at the same tolerance}: interacting only with
nearest neighbours lets locally cohesive groups stabilise before global
consensus propagates. This is the one result here that is neither a scale
artefact nor a restatement of the input distribution.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{../figures/fig12_opinion_dynamics.png}
\caption{Deffuant--Weisbuch dynamics seeded on E03. Left: final cluster count
against confidence threshold $\varepsilon$ for the $k$NN respondent network, a
complete graph, and a jittered-seed control. The flat $5.00\pm0.00$ plateau
below $\varepsilon=0.25$ is forced by the five-point grid and vanishes under

\end{figure*}

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What the data supports}

\paragraph{Superficial consensus, genuine local structure.}
The headline is not polarisation. Agreement runs at $79.8\%$ across every block
and PC1 explains about $8\%$ of variance, so there are no two opposed camps.
There is a broad agreeable consensus with real disagreement concentrated in
identifiable places.

\paragraph{Disagreement is domain-specific, not dispositional.}
Environment and society are settled; technology and education are not. Within
education the cohort is precise about what it rejects --- compulsory attendance
and written examinations --- while endorsing curriculum reform above $94\%$.
Within technology the objection tracks delegated consequential judgment
(diagnosis, autonomous vehicles) rather than automation as such: robots in
hazardous work still score $82.4\%$.

\paragraph{Most apparent structure was response style.}
Row-centring removes $87\%$ of the item pairs exceeding $|r|\geq0.30$. Any
analysis skipping this step finds dense, uniformly positive structure and is
measuring agreeableness.

\paragraph{Opinions are internally consistent.}
Structural balance (Figure~\ref{fig:g11}) rises monotonically to $100\%$ of
triads as weak edges are removed, against a sign-permutation null flat at
$50\%$. A spurious effect would not sharpen as noise is filtered out.

\paragraph{Communities ignore the survey's own design.}
The 15 detected communities align poorly with the four thematic blocks
(NMI $=0.205$). Respondents associate ideas across topic boundaries, not within
the categories the questionnaire was built from.

\subsection{What failed, and what that tells us}
\label{sec:nulls}

Three results did not come out the way the analysis was set up to expect. They
are reported because each one changes what may legitimately be claimed.

\paragraph{1. Most of the correlation matrix is noise.}
Marchenko--Pastur filtering (Section~\ref{sec:g5}) leaves exactly four
eigenvalue modes above the band, carrying $25.6\%$ of total variance. The other
$74.4\%$ is what $91$ respondents answering $60$ items generate by chance. Any
claim resting on a single correlation, or on fine structure of the full matrix,
is unsupported --- which is why every downstream network is built from the
denoised matrix rather than the raw one.

\paragraph{2. The community structure does not survive its own null.}
This is the sharpest negative result, and which null is used decides it. A
correlation-derived network has transitivity built into its construction: if A
correlates with B and B with C, A and C are pulled toward correlation
automatically, and that alone manufactures modularity in structureless data.
Rewiring \cite{maslov2002specificity} and Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi nulls take the
\emph{finished graph} as given, so neither can detect structure the
construction itself created. Only a column-permutation null --- destroying
association in the raw responses, then regenerating the entire chain of centre,
correlate, threshold, measure --- tests the claim being made. Both networks were
tested independently and both return the same verdict:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
Network & Null & $Q_{\text{null}}$ & $z$ \\
\midrule
Respondent & Rewiring & $0.337\pm.011$ & $7.46$ \\
($Q=0.421$) & Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi & $0.337\pm.011$ & $7.28$ \\
            & \textbf{Permutation} & $0.404\pm.018$ & $\mathbf{0.91}$ \\
\addlinespace
Item & Rewiring & $0.333\pm.013$ & $12.13$ \\
($Q=0.491$) & Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi & $0.420\pm.016$ & $4.46$ \\
            & \textbf{Permutation} & $0.629\pm.044$ & $\mathbf{-3.14}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Against the graph-level nulls the structure looks overwhelming, at $z$ up to
$12$. Against the null that reproduces the construction it vanishes on the
respondent network ($p=0.19$) and \emph{inverts} on the item network: scrambled
data pushed through the identical pipeline yields more modular graphs than the
real data. Had we stopped at the conventional rewiring null we would have
reported a strong, entirely spurious finding. The communities are real as a
description of the graph; they are not evidence about the cohort.

\paragraph{3. The dimension count depends on who you keep.}
Filling the same $242$ cells by column mean, column median or ordinal
regression all yield exactly four modes ($24.7\%$, $25.0\%$, $25.6\%$ of
variance) --- the structure is a property of the data, not of the imputer. But
dropping the ten respondents with more than twelve missing items and filling the
rest with a column statistic gives only \emph{three} modes at $19.3\%$. The
count is robust to \emph{how} missing cells are filled and fragile to
\emph{which} respondents are retained. Keeping all 91 is the better-supported
choice --- discarding usable respondents to avoid modelling $4.4\%$ of cells
trades away more than it protects --- but the result is conditional on it.

\subsection{Methods tried and rejected}
\label{sec:rejected}

Alternatives were measured rather than argued about.

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{p{0.30\columnwidth}p{0.62\columnwidth}}
\toprule
Approach & Why it was rejected \\
\midrule
Recode NC as Neutral
& Imposes a $0.92$-point downward shift at those cells; asserts a response that
was explicitly withheld. \\
\addlinespace
Drop the 17 NC users
& Discards a fifth of the sample to remove 37 cells, and is the only NC
treatment that disturbs the recovered partition (AMI $0.337$). \\
\addlinespace
Mutual $k$NN (respondents)
& Shatters the graph: 66 edges, 37 components, giant component $49.5\%$. Union
$k$NN keeps one component. \\
\addlinespace
Raw uncentred similarity
& $624$ of $1{,}770$ pairs survive $|r|>0.22$, $83\%$ of them positive --- a
$35\%$-dense graph measuring agreeableness. \\
\addlinespace
Percentile threshold (p90)
& Fixes the edge count by construction, so it cannot say whether any edge beats
chance. On respondents it leaves 22 components and 20 isolates. \\
\addlinespace
Louvain for communities
& Superseded by Leiden: its greedy phase can leave communities internally
disconnected \cite{traag2019louvain}; Leiden improves $Q$ from $0.472$ to
$0.491$ at the same $k=15$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Limitations}

Items with means above roughly $4.5$ measure social desirability more than
preference strength, and with few reverse-worded items acquiescence and a
genuine ceiling cannot be fully separated. The sample is $n=96$ from a single
institution, so only large gaps between statements sit safely above sampling
variation, and monotone dropout leaves later blocks on a slightly smaller base.
No demographic fields exist, so nothing can be attributed to a subgroup.
Finally, and most importantly for the network claims: correlation stability
does not imply partition stability. Across NC treatments the correlation
matrices agree at $r>0.94$ while their community assignments agree at AMI as
low as $0.337$. Community-level conclusions need their own bootstrap stability
analysis before being treated as findings.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We built an end-to-end pipeline from a raw ordinal questionnaire to a signed
opinion network and a process on it, measuring each intervention rather than
asserting it: ordinal-regression recovery of $242$ cells beats a modal baseline
under both masking mechanisms, and row-centring eliminates $87\%$ of item pairs
correlated at $|r|\geq0.30$. A permutation null puts the noise floor at $0.106$
against an observed spread of $0.139$, Marchenko--Pastur filtering leaves four
modes carrying $25.6\%$ of variance, and the threshold came from a $46$-point
sweep by a stated rule at $|r|>0.22$.

The same framework produced the most useful negative result. Community
structure clears rewiring and Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi nulls on \emph{both} networks
and fails the permutation null on both --- a finding we would have missed
entirely had we used only the conventional null, and the clearest argument in
this report for choosing a null that reproduces the construction. What survives
is a cohort in superficial consensus with genuine but local structure: four
modest cross-cutting dimensions, communities that ignore the survey's own
thematic design (NMI $0.205$), and a signed topology that becomes perfectly
balanced as noise is stripped away. Not two camps.
