\section{Dataset Documentation}
\label{sec:data}
% ============================================================

The questionnaire contains 60 items in four blocks of 15 --- technology
(T01--T15), education (E01--E15), society and ethics (S01--S15) and environment
(V01--V15) --- answered by 96 respondents. Five ordered categories are encoded
symmetrically as $\text{SD}=-2$ through $\text{SA}=+2$, which represents the
ordering without asserting equal spacing; a sixth option, \emph{No Comments}
(NC), is treated as missing. Internal consistency varies sharply by block:
Cronbach's $\alpha$ is $0.87$ for environment and $0.82$ for society but only
$0.65$ for technology and $0.58$ for education.

\subsection{The response ceiling}
\label{sec:ceiling}

The most consequential property of this dataset is its ceiling.
Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling} shows $79.8\%$ of valid answers on the agree side,
$43.1\%$ \emph{Strongly Agree}, and only $7.0\%$ disagreeing. The grand mean is
$\mathbf{+1.14}$ on the $[-2,+2]$ scale. Two consequences drive every later
decision: item means barely discriminate, so the signal is in \emph{which}
statements attract disagreement; and two respondents look similar under any raw
similarity measure simply by both being agreeable.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{../figures/fig_response_ceiling.png}
\caption{\textbf{Response ceiling and block-level agreement.} (a) All
$5{,}218$ valid answers: nearly four in five are on the agree side.
(b) Agreement profile per block with block mean and Cronbach's $\alpha$.
Environment and society are near consensus; education carries the most
disagreement and the weakest consistency.}
\label{fig:ceiling}
\end{figure*}

The ceiling is not uniform. Environment ($\mu=4.39$ on the $1$--$5$ scale,
$89.5\%$ agree) and society ($\mu=4.28$) are near consensus, while technology
($\mu=3.99$) and education ($\mu=3.91$, $13.8\%$ disagree) retain real
disagreement --- and those two blocks also have the weakest internal
consistency, consistent with their items tapping several distinct opinions
rather than one settled disposition.

Education and technology contribute most of the twelve most contested items;
environment contributes none. Compulsory attendance (E03, $70.1\%$
disagreeing) is the only statement the cohort actively rejects, yet the same
cohort agrees at $94.3\%$ that curricula should be updated more often --- the
objection is to assessment and teaching format, not to the institution.
Technology shows the same pattern: AI-assisted diagnosis and autonomous
vehicles are its least popular items, while teaching AI and robots in
hazardous work both score above $79\%$, so the objection tracks delegating
consequential judgment rather than automation itself.

\subsection{Missing data and how it was recovered}
\label{sec:imputation}

Of 96 records, 85 are complete, 5 are entirely empty, and 6 stop partway. The
five empty records are dropped, leaving \textbf{91 respondents}; none is
dropped merely for being incomplete. That leaves $205$ structural blanks plus
$37$ NC cells, $\mathbf{242}$ in total. Dropout is \emph{monotone} --- a
truncated tail, not scattered gaps --- so later blocks rest on a slightly
smaller base.

\paragraph{NC is not Neutral.} Recoding a withheld answer as Neutral would
assert a response nobody gave: a simple model fitted on the clean responses
predicts $3.92$ at the NC cells, not $3$, so recoding would impose a
systematic downward shift. NC use also shows no pattern by item or by
respondent, so there is no basis for assigning it any fixed category.

\paragraph{Recovery by ordinal regression.} Each missing cell is predicted from
a respondent's other 59 answers by a regularised ordinal regression fitted per
item, which respects the order of the five response categories without
assuming equal spacing between them (the full method is in
Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}). We validate this by artificial masking under two
mechanisms --- scattered cells and a structural tail, mimicking NC and
drop-out respectively --- and compare against a simple modal-guess baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:val}).

\begin{table}[t]

\label{tab:val}

One caution carries forward: different ways of handling NC give correlation
matrices that agree closely ($r>0.94$) but community assignments that can
disagree sharply (AMI as low as $0.337$). \emph{A stable correlation matrix
does not guarantee a stable partition of it} --- a point
Section~\ref{sec:nulls} returns to.

\subsection{Row-centring}
\label{sec:centring}

Imputation yields a complete $91\times 60$ matrix, but not one that can be
correlated directly: with a grand mean of $+1.14$, a respondent who agrees with
everything raises all 60 entries together, which reads as association between
every pair of items at once. We replace each entry by its deviation from that
respondent's own mean,
\begin{equation}
x'_{i,j} = x_{i,j} - \bar{x}_{i\cdot},
\qquad
\bar{x}_{i\cdot} = \tfrac{1}{60}\textstyle\sum_{k} x_{i,k}.
\end{equation}
Centring is applied strictly after imputation and strictly before correlation.
Note that rank correlation does \emph{not} remove this bias automatically:
Spearman is invariant to a monotone transform of a column, not to an additive
shift that differs by row. Graph 3 (Section~\ref{sec:g3}) measures how much it
matters.

\section{Pipeline Followed}
\label{sec:pipeline}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What the network is}
\label{sec:network}

\textbf{Vertices are the 60 questionnaire items}, and \textbf{edges are signed
correlations between items}, computed on the row-centred matrix and retained
with their sign. Edges are weighted by default and binarised only where a
metric requires it (path lengths, triad counts).

Items were chosen over respondents deliberately: an item network is thresholded
directly on a measured quantity and every vertex carries an interpretable
label, whereas a respondent network has no directly measured edge and would
have to be constructed by an imposed neighbourhood rule. Signs are kept because
a negative edge --- two questions dividing the cohort in opposite directions
--- is a stronger statement than mere association; Graph 8
(Section~\ref{sec:g8}) shows this sign pattern separating topic blocks cleanly
once acquiescence is removed.

\subsection{Step by step}
\label{sec:steps}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt \parskip0pt
\item \textbf{Parse and encode.} Identify the 60 item columns, map the five
categories to $\{-2,\dots,+2\}$ and NC to \texttt{NaN}. Yields $96\times60$
with $542$ missing cells.

\item \textbf{Filter respondents.} Drop only the five entirely empty records.
$91\times60$, $242$ missing cells.

\item \textbf{Impute} (Section~\ref{sec:imputation}). One regularised
proportional-odds ordinal logistic regression per item, predicting it from the
other 59.

\item \textbf{Row-centre} (Section~\ref{sec:centring}). Subtract each
respondent's own mean across all 60 items --- strictly after imputation,
strictly before correlation.

\item \textbf{Correlate.} Pearson correlation over the 60 columns, giving a
$60\times60$ signed matrix with $\binom{60}{2}=1{,}770$ distinct off-diagonal
entries.

\item \textbf{Establish the noise floor} (Section~\ref{sec:g4}). Re-run steps
4--5 on $200$ independently column-permuted copies of the matrix from step 3.

\item \textbf{Denoise} (Section~\ref{sec:g5}). Keep only the eigenvalue modes
exceeding the Marchenko--Pastur upper edge $\lambda_+$, rebuild the matrix from
those modes, and restore the unit diagonal.

\item \textbf{Threshold} (Section~\ref{sec:g7}). Sweep all $46$ cutoffs from
$0.05$ to $0.50$ and select by a stated rule rather than by inspecting the
result. Selects $|r|>0.22$.

\item \textbf{Measure} (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). Community detection
(Graphs 6 and 9) and topic-block connectivity (Graph 8) --- each compared
against an explicit null.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Analysis and Visualisations}
\label{sec:analysis}
% ============================================================

This section reports the nine analyses. Each states what the figure shows,
what was found, and what follows from it.

\paragraph{A note on respondent counts.}
All figures below report $n=91$: every non-empty respondent is retained and the
missing cells are recovered by ordinal regression
(Section~\ref{sec:imputation}). An alternative pipeline that instead drops
respondents with more than 12 missing items, leaving 86, is examined in
Section~\ref{sec:nulls}.

\subsection{Graph 1: Missingness map and response distribution}
\label{sec:g1}

Figure~\ref{fig:g1} shows every cell of the raw $96\times60$ matrix as missing
or present, respondents sorted by missing count, beside the distribution of
per-respondent means.

\textbf{Findings.} The $542$ missing cells ($505$ structural blanks plus $37$
NC) are extremely unevenly distributed: five entirely empty respondents account
for $300$ of them, and the remaining $242$ spread thinly across the other 91
--- a rate of $4.4\%$. Below the cutoff the map is almost clean, with two
visible tails from respondents who stopped early. The right panel locates the
grand mean at $1.14$, with only one respondent below zero.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{../figures/fig01_missingness.png}
\caption{\textbf{Missingness map and per-respondent response means.} Left:
missingness over all 96 raw respondents, sorted by missing-item count. The
dashed line marks the exclusion rule --- only the five entirely empty records
are dropped, and the remaining 91 have their $242$ missing cells imputed.
Right: per-respondent mean over answered items, grand mean $1.14$.}
\label{fig:g1}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Implication.} This justifies the retention policy directly: all but
five respondents answered at least $80\%$ of items, so there is no population
of badly incomplete respondents whose removal would simplify anything, and a
count-based exclusion rule would discard usable respondents to remove very few
cells. The right panel is the first direct evidence of the acquiescence problem
that row-centring corrects.

\subsection{Graph 2: Item variance ranking}
\label{sec:g2}

Figure~\ref{fig:g2} ranks all 60 items by standard deviation on the
\emph{uncentred} matrix. Centring is deliberately not applied here: it changes
each item's variance and would answer a different question (spread after
removing each respondent's baseline) than the one asked (how divided the cohort
is on the statement as posed).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\columnwidth]{../figures/fig02_item_variance.png}
\caption{\textbf{Item variance across the four survey blocks.} All 60 items by
standard deviation on the uncentred matrix, coloured by block. Contested items
concentrate in education and technology; environment clusters at the consensus
end.}
\label{fig:g2}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Findings.} Item SD ranges from $0.50$ (E15, near-unanimous) to $1.18$
(E04, online learning). The five most contested are E04, E03, E02, T08, T07. By
block mean SD: technology $0.92$, education $0.88$, society $0.79$, environment
$0.72$.

\textbf{Implication.} Technology has the highest average variance, but the
\emph{sharpest} disagreements are educational --- three of the five most
divided items are E02--E04, all about assessment and teaching format --- and
environment contributes none of the top ten. This is a property of the
instrument, not of preprocessing: the questionnaire contains one settled
domain, one moderately settled, and two live ones. Consensus items carry little
information for a correlation network, since an item everyone endorses cannot
covary with anything, so the signal is necessarily concentrated in the
contested tail.

\subsection{Graph 3: The effect of row-centring}
\label{sec:g3}

Figure~\ref{fig:g3} overlays all $\binom{60}{2}=1{,}770$ pairwise item
correlations before and after row-centring, on identical bin edges.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\columnwidth]{../figures/fig03_row_centring.png}
\caption{\textbf{Effect of row-centring on pairwise item correlations.} All
$1{,}770$ pairs before and after centring, shared bin edges. Centring moves the
mean from $0.151$ to $-0.004$ and removes $87\%$ of pairs exceeding
$|r|\geq0.30$.}
\label{fig:g3}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Findings.} The raw distribution is displaced positive (mean and median
both $0.151$), with almost no pair appreciably negative. After centring it sits
on zero (mean $-0.004$, median $-0.008$) with positive skew $0.221$, leaving a
heavier right tail of genuinely co-varying pairs. At $|r|\geq0.30$ the
surviving pairs fall from $\mathbf{327}$ to $\mathbf{42}$ --- a reduction of
$87\%$.

\textbf{Implication.} This converts the centring argument from an assertion
into a measurement: seven eighths of the apparent association in the raw matrix
is shared response style. Built on raw scores the network would have been
dense, almost entirely positive, and would have supported confident but
spurious conclusions. The emergence of negative correlations after centring ---
invisible in the raw distribution --- is also what makes the sign pattern in
Graph 8's topic-block analysis interpretable at all.

\subsection{Graph 4: Real versus shuffled correlations}
\label{sec:g4}

Every analysis below runs on the same $91$-respondent, ordinal-imputed matrix,
so no part of this report silently uses a different sample.

Figure~\ref{fig:g4} compares the $1{,}770$ observed item--item correlations
against a null that destroys inter-item association while preserving every
item's marginal exactly. Each of $200$ replicates permutes \emph{every column
independently} and re-runs the full pipeline --- row-centre, then correlate ---
on the shuffled matrix. This detail is not cosmetic: one permutation applied to
all columns, or permuting rows, merely relabels respondents and leaves the
correlation structure numerically untouched.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{../figures/fig04_noise_floor.png}
\caption{\textbf{Observed versus permuted item correlations.} (a) Observed
correlations against a $200$-replicate permutation null that re-runs the whole
pipeline on independently shuffled columns; SD $0.139$ against $0.106$, the
excess confined to the tails. (b) Surviving edges against edges expected from
sampling scatter alone (log scale); the shaded band is the excess over chance.}
\label{fig:g4}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Findings.} Correlations from $n=91$ respondents scatter about zero with
SD $1/\sqrt{n-3}=0.107$ even when nothing is related. The permuted null
reproduces this at $\mathbf{0.106}$, against $\mathbf{0.139}$ observed. Real
structure exists but is modest, and lives in the tails:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
$|r|>$ & $0.15$ & $0.20$ & $0.25$ & $0.30$ \\
\midrule
Observed edges & $502$ & $266$ & $124$ & $51$ \\
Expected by chance & $286$ & $107$ & $32$ & $7$ \\
Chance fraction & $57\%$ & $40\%$ & $26\%$ & $14\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Implication.} At the conventional cutoff $|r|>0.15$ more than half the
surviving edges are what $1{,}770$ simultaneous estimates from $91$ people
produce by luck. A network drawn there is mostly noise, and no downstream
community detection can separate the two after the fact. This makes threshold
selection a measured trade-off rather than a convention, and supplies the
chance curve that Graph 7 is read against.

\subsection{Graph 5: How many dimensions are real?}
\label{sec:g5}

Graph 4 shows a noise floor exists, but not how much of the matrix survives
it. A plain PCA cannot answer this either: the first component explains only
$8.3\%$ of variance, which is just as consistent with weak real structure as
with none. Marchenko--Pastur theory gives an exact answer instead: for a
correlation matrix built from pure noise, the eigenvalues fall inside a known
band, $\lambda_+=(1+1/\sqrt{Q})^2$ with $Q=T/N$; anything above that band is
structure $T$ respondents could not produce by chance alone.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{../figures/fig05_eigenvalue_spectrum.png}
\caption{\textbf{Eigenvalue spectrum against the Marchenko--Pastur noise band.}
The spectrum of the row-centred item correlation matrix against the
Marchenko--Pastur density for pure noise of the same shape ($T=91$, $N=60$).
Exactly four eigenvalues exceed $\lambda_+=3.283$, carrying $25.6\%$ of
variance. Inset: a clear gap between the fourth ($3.35$) and fifth ($2.86$).}
\label{fig:g5}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Findings.} With $91$ respondents and $60$ items, the band's upper edge
is $\lambda_+=\mathbf{3.283}$. Exactly four eigenvalues clear it --- $4.38$,
$4.06$, $3.55$, $3.35$ --- carrying $\mathbf{25.6\%}$ of the total variance,
while the fifth, at $2.86$, sits clearly inside the band (Figure~\ref{fig:g5}).
Two of the four modes align with the survey's own topic blocks; the other two
cut across them, pointing to attitudes that do not respect the four-topic
design.

\textbf{Implication.} The remaining three quarters of the matrix is sampling
noise. Every network built later in this report uses only these four modes, not
the raw matrix, so it reflects only what $91$ respondents can actually
support.

\subsection{Graph 6: Reordered correlation heatmap}
\label{sec:g6}

Figure~\ref{fig:g6} presents the MP-denoised correlation matrix reordered by
the community assignments detected in Graph 9 (Section~\ref{sec:g9}). A
diverging colour map centred at zero distinguishes positive from negative
correlations, and the marginal colour strips mark the predefined thematic
blocks (technology, education, society/ethics, environment).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{../figures/fig06_reordered_heatmap.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{MP-denoised item correlation matrix reordered by community.}
The 15 communities are separated by black lines, with topic blocks marked in
the margins. The communities cut across the predefined topic blocks, showing
that respondents' association patterns do not follow the survey's own design.}
\label{fig:g6}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Findings.} The block-diagonal structure of the reordered matrix
confirms that the community detection groups densely connected items. The
marginal strips, however, show those data-driven communities cutting directly
across the survey's predefined thematic blocks, and the formal comparison
yields a normalised mutual information (NMI) of $0.205$.

\textbf{Implication.} Respondents' association patterns do not partition neatly
into the four hypothesised domains. The partition is a good description of the
graph, but it is not the partition the instrument was built around.

\subsection{Graph 7: Choosing the correlation threshold}
\label{sec:g7}

Picking a single threshold invites the objection that it was chosen to produce
the result. Figure~\ref{fig:g7} answers this by rebuilding the network at all
$46$ cutoffs from $0.05$ to $0.50$ and tracking edge count, the giant
component, fragmentation and modularity at each one, so the chosen value is
visibly one point on a curve rather than a cherry-pick.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{../figures/fig07_threshold_sweep.png}
\caption{\textbf{Threshold sweep and network fragmentation.} The signed item
network rebuilt at all $46$ cutoffs from $0.05$ to $0.50$. (a) Edge count
against the chance expectation from Graph 4. (b) Giant component.
(c) Components and isolates. (d) Louvain modularity, which reaches $0.72$ only
once the graph has broken into $43$ pieces. The vertical line marks the
selected $|r|>0.22$.}
\label{fig:g7}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Findings.} Two things pull in opposite directions as the threshold
rises: the share of chance edges from Graph 4 falls, but the graph eventually
breaks apart --- by $|r|>0.27$ the giant component covers under half the
items. We pick the highest cutoff at which the giant component still spans two
thirds of the items and mean degree stays above $4$: this selects
$\boldsymbol{|r|>0.22}$, giving $\mathbf{131}$ edges ($68$ positive, $63$
negative), mean degree $4.37$, and a giant component of $50$ of $60$ items
($83\%$). At the next step, $|r|>0.23$, the giant component no longer
qualifies. An independent estimate from Graph 4's chance-edge count agrees
closely: about $138$ real edges expected there, against $131$ kept here.

\textbf{Implication.} Modularity keeps climbing past this point, reaching
$0.72$ --- but only once the graph has broken into $43$ disconnected pieces.
Quoting that number alone, without the rest of the sweep, would mistake
fragmentation for community structure, the exact trap Section~\ref{sec:nulls}
unpacks.

\subsection{Graph 8: Topic-block connectivity}
\label{sec:g8}

The questionnaire arrives with its own partition --- fifteen items each for
technology, education, society and environment --- which is an editorial claim
about what belongs together. Whether it is also a \emph{statistical} one should
be asked before any community result is interpreted against it. We collapse the
$60\times60$ correlation matrix into a $4\times4$ block matrix, averaging the
$105$ \emph{distinct} within-block pairs on the diagonal (self-correlations
excluded: keeping the unit diagonal would manufacture the very block structure
being tested). Significance comes from a \textbf{block-label permutation} ---
the matrix is held fixed and the T/E/S/V labels reshuffled over $5{,}000$
seeded draws --- which needs no threshold and is immune to any constant offset
in the matrix.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/fig09a_block_matrices.png}
\caption{\textbf{Topic-block connectivity before and after row-centring.} Mean
item--item correlation within and between blocks. \textbf{Left:} the raw
matrix --- every cell positive, the design invisible under acquiescence.
\textbf{Right:} the same collapse after row-centring, shared colour scale;
diagonal positive, all off-diagonals negative.}
\label{fig:g8a}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\columnwidth]{../figures/fig09b_block_permutation.png}
\caption{\textbf{Is the block design statistically real?} Distribution of the
within-minus-between correlation gap under $5{,}000$ reshufflings of the block
labels. The observed gap, $+0.1027$ ($z=13.4$), is nowhere near this
shuffled-label distribution.}
\label{fig:g8b}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Findings.} Row-centring is what makes the design visible at all. On the
raw matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:g8a}, left) all sixteen cells are positive ($+0.041$
to $+0.377$): acquiescence makes every topic look connected to every other.
After centring, the sign pattern separates cleanly --- \textbf{every diagonal
cell positive, every off-diagonal negative}. Within-block mean $r=+0.0746$
against between-block $-0.0282$ gives a gap of $\mathbf{+0.1027}$ against a
null of $-0.00003\pm0.0077$: $\mathbf{z=13.4}$, with no permutation in
$5{,}000$ reaching the observed value ($p<0.0002$).

The blocks are not equally coherent: environment $+0.136$, technology $+0.066$,
education $+0.054$, society/ethics $+0.042$. Environment is more than three
times as internally consistent as ethics. S--V is the one seam, at $-0.004$ the
only pair not clearly separated and the strongest off-diagonal cell in the raw
matrix ($+0.256$).

\textbf{Implication.} The thematic design is statistically real, so a later
community that aligns with a block is recovering something the instrument built
in, while one that cuts across blocks is the more informative object --- which
is exactly what Graph 9 finds (NMI $0.205$). The uneven diagonal sharpens this:
environmental attitudes travel as a package, whereas ethics items are close to
independent, so respondents hold ethical positions issue by issue rather than
as a doctrine. One caveat: the negative off-diagonals do \emph{not} mean topics
oppose one another. Row-centring forces each row to sum to zero, pushing the
grand mean of all $1{,}770$ correlations to $-0.0038$; the values should be
read relative to the diagonal, not as opposition.

\subsection{Graph 9: Are the communities real?}
\label{sec:g9}

Figure~\ref{fig:g9alg} compares three community-detection algorithms on the
item network at $|r|>0.22$. Figure~\ref{fig:g9} then checks the communities
found against three random comparisons --- degree-preserving rewiring, an
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph, and a column-permutation baseline that scrambles
responses independently per item --- to see whether the same result could
appear by chance.

\begin{figure*}[tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{../figures/fig10b_algorithm_comparison.pdf}

(a) Peak modularity per algorithm; all converge on $k=15$, Leiden highest at

\textbf{Which algorithm.} Louvain found 15 communities at $Q=0.472$, but its

a property of the graph rather than of one algorithm; Leiden is used from here

\textbf{But the comparisons disagree.} Leiden's $Q=0.491$ comfortably beats the
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi comparison ($0.420\pm0.016$) and the rewiring comparison
($0.333\pm0.013$). Against the column-permutation comparison, though, it does
not just fail --- it scores \emph{lower}: $0.629\pm0.044$ against our $0.491$.
Data with the responses scrambled, pushed through the exact same pipeline,
produces more modular graphs than the real data does. Section~\ref{sec:nulls}
explains why: the first two comparisons only reshuffle the finished graph, so
neither can catch structure the graph-building process itself creates; only
the column-permutation comparison rebuilds that process from scratch.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What the data supports}
\label{sec:supports}

\paragraph{Superficial consensus, genuine local structure.}
The headline is not polarisation. Agreement runs at $79.8\%$ across every block
and PC1 explains about $8\%$ of variance, so there are no two opposed camps.
There is a broad agreeable consensus with real disagreement concentrated in
identifiable places.

\paragraph{Disagreement is domain-specific, not dispositional.}
Environment and society are settled; technology and education are not. Within
education the cohort is precise about what it rejects --- compulsory attendance
and written examinations --- while endorsing curriculum reform above $94\%$.
Within technology the objection tracks delegated consequential judgment
(diagnosis, autonomous vehicles) rather than automation as such: robots in
hazardous work still score $82.4\%$.

\paragraph{Most apparent structure was response style.}
Row-centring removes $87\%$ of the item pairs exceeding $|r|\geq0.30$. Any
analysis skipping this step finds dense, uniformly positive structure and is
measuring agreeableness.

\paragraph{Communities ignore the survey's own design.}
The thematic blocks are statistically real ($z=13.4$ on the
within-minus-between gap, Graph 8), yet the 15 detected communities align with
them only at NMI $=0.205$. Respondents associate ideas across topic boundaries,
not within the categories the questionnaire was built from.

\subsection{What failed, and what that tells us}
\label{sec:nulls}

Three results did not come out the way the analysis was set up to expect. They
are reported because each one changes what may legitimately be claimed.

\paragraph{1. Most of the correlation matrix is noise.}
Marchenko--Pastur filtering (Section~\ref{sec:g5}) leaves exactly four
eigenvalue modes above the band, carrying $25.6\%$ of total variance. The other
$74.4\%$ is what $91$ respondents answering $60$ items generate by chance. Any
claim resting on a single correlation, or on fine structure of the full matrix,
is unsupported --- which is why every downstream network is built from the
denoised matrix rather than the raw one.

\paragraph{2. The community structure does not survive its own null.}
This is the sharpest negative result, and which null is used decides it. A
correlation-derived network has transitivity built into its construction: if A
correlates with B and B with C, A and C are pulled toward correlation
automatically, and that alone manufactures modularity in structureless data.
Rewiring and Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi nulls take the \emph{finished graph} as given,
so neither can detect structure the construction itself created. Only a
column-permutation null --- destroying association in the raw responses, then
regenerating the entire chain of centre, correlate, threshold, measure --- puts
the claim actually being made under test. The three nulls disagree sharply:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Null model & $Q_{\text{null}}$ & $z$ \\
\midrule
Degree-preserving rewiring & $0.333\pm.013$ & $12.13$ \\
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi & $0.420\pm.016$ & $4.46$ \\
\textbf{Column permutation} & $0.629\pm.044$ & $\mathbf{-3.14}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Against the graph-level nulls the observed $Q=0.491$ looks overwhelming, at $z$
up to $12$. Against the null that reproduces the construction it \emph{inverts}:
scrambled data pushed through the identical pipeline yields more modular graphs
than the real data. Had we stopped at the conventional rewiring null we would
have reported a strong, entirely spurious finding. The communities are real as
a description of the graph; they are not evidence about the cohort.

\paragraph{3. The dimension count depends on who you keep.}
Filling the same $242$ cells by column mean, column median or ordinal
regression all yield exactly four modes ($24.7\%$, $25.0\%$, $25.6\%$ of
variance) --- the structure is a property of the data, not of the imputer. But
dropping the ten respondents with more than twelve missing items and filling
the rest with a column statistic gives only \emph{three} modes at $19.3\%$. The
count is robust to \emph{how} missing cells are filled and fragile to
\emph{which} respondents are retained. Keeping all 91 is the better-supported
choice --- discarding usable respondents to avoid modelling $4.4\%$ of cells
trades away more than it protects --- but the result is conditional on it.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We built an end-to-end pipeline from a raw ordinal questionnaire to a signed
item network, measuring each intervention rather than asserting it.
Ordinal-regression recovery of the $242$ missing cells beats a modal baseline
under both masking mechanisms, and row-centring eliminates $87\%$ of the item
pairs correlated at $|r|\geq0.30$. A permutation null puts the noise floor at
$0.106$ against an observed spread of $0.139$, Marchenko--Pastur filtering
leaves four significant modes carrying $25.6\%$ of variance, and the threshold
came from a $46$-point sweep by a stated rule at $|r|>0.22$.

On the resulting network, the questionnaire's own thematic design proves
statistically real ($z=13.4$), yet the 15 detected communities nonetheless cut
across the four topic blocks (NMI $0.205$).

The same framework produced the most useful negative result. That community
structure clears the rewiring and Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi nulls but fails the
column-permutation null --- a finding we would have missed entirely had we used
only the conventional null, and the clearest argument in this report for
choosing a null that reproduces the construction.
